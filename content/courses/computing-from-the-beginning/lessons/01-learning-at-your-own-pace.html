Start with curiosity, not experience

Find your way through a lesson and explain one thing you have learned.

Before you begin: no previous computing knowledge is expected. Paper and conversation are enough.

What this course asks of you

You do not need to be a programmer, to own a powerful computer, or to know technical words. This course begins with everyday actions: asking a question, giving an instruction, keeping a note and checking a result. You may read alone or with someone who reads aloud. You can answer by speaking, drawing, pointing or writing. Age does not decide which topic you are ready for.

A computer is a machine that follows instructions to work with information. Information might be a word, a picture, a sound or a number. Later we will call stored information data. A course is a connected route through a subject. A lesson is one part of that route. An exercise is a small activity that lets you try an idea. None of those words describes a test of your worth or intelligence.

How to use a page

Each lesson says what you are learning, introduces words before using them in an activity, gives a worked example, and offers a question with an explanation. Read one section at a time. When a word is unfamiliar, pause at its first explanation or visit the course word list. Going back is part of learning.

A link is text or an image that opens another page when selected. In a browser, the Back control usually returns to the page you just left. A browser is an application used to read websites; an application is a program made for a particular activity. A program is a set of instructions a computer can carry out. The course contents page lists all the lessons. Previous and Next links follow this course; Related learning links let you explore a subject more deeply.

A small learning record

Imagine you are learning to send a greeting. Before trying, you predict that typing a name will show it on the screen. You try, look at what happened, and compare it with your prediction. If nothing appears, the useful question is what happened between your action and the result. It is not whether you are a good learner.

Keep three short notes: what I expected, what I observed, and what I would try next. Observed means noticed directly, rather than guessed. A drawing or a spoken recording can be your note. Keep it on paper or in a private file. This course does not require an account, a public profile or sharing personal information.

Choose a comfortable way to learn

You may use browser zoom to make text larger, a screen reader that speaks text, or a printed page. The written exercises do not depend on seeing colours or on finishing quickly. If you share a device, ask its owner before changing or saving anything. A parent, carer, friend, teacher or librarian can help you operate a device without doing the thinking for you.

No installation or command is needed for this lesson. If a later practical step is unavailable on your device, complete its paper version first. Reading a concept and running a program are different activities; you can make progress with either.

Try it step by step

  1. Choose an everyday task, such as putting a book on a shelf.
  2. Say or draw what you expect to happen when someone follows your instruction.
  3. Ask what could make the instruction unclear: which book, which shelf, or which side?
  4. Record one more precise instruction and one question you still have.

Check your understanding

If you return to an earlier lesson, have you failed to make progress?

No. You have identified something you want to understand better. Explaining a word in your own way is useful progress. The order is a guide, not a race.

Before you continue

You are ready to continue when you can find the course contents and name one question you want to investigate.
