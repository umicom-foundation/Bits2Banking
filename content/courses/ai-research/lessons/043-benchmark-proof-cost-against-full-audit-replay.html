Research-grounded native computing, verifiable evidence and distributed-systems boundaries.

What you will learn

Explain benchmark sparse inclusion against full audit replay with explicit assumptions, an independently checkable worked example and the limits of the evidence.

Concept and mechanism

An inclusion proof is compact evidence that a digest was committed to a root. Full audit replay validates actual receipt semantics, signatures and possibly training arithmetic. The correct experiment reports the fraction of work verified and residual attack surface. Selective proofs should be combined with random audits only where the security-cost trade-off can be modelled and tested.

Worked example

One thousand records, one inclusion check versus full replay: a short proof saves digest transfer but does not re-execute 1,000 training updates. Record separate CPU time and uncovered risks.

Try it yourself

Propose a budgeted verifier experiment varying audit sampling 1%, 10% and 100%.

Reveal the fully worked answer

State detection probability assumptions and actual measured verifier time; the Merkle primitive alone is insufficient.

Where the proof stops

Work-sampling challenges can miss cheating and cannot be described as complete correctness certification.

A native test can verify byte-level membership but cannot by itself authenticate a participant, authorise financial transactions, establish consensus or prove neural model improvement. Record the independent root, protocol and unperformed tests.

Native implementation boundary

Use the existing Umicom Framework C23 SHA-256 and ordered record chain. The opt-in Merkle service consumes validated digests and exposes bounded membership checks. Existing Python and PowerShell teaching tools remain alternatives, not the implementation.
