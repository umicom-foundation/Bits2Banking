1. Money, banks and the first question in treasury

Bits to Banking · Treasury Management, TMS and Calypso From Zero · Umicom Foundation

Imagine a small community kitchen. It buys food, pays electricity bills and receives donations. The kitchen may have a useful purpose and careful volunteers, but it cannot pay a supplier using a promise that somebody might donate next week. It needs money available when the bill is due.

This is where our journey into treasury begins. Before we discuss computer systems, we need to understand what money does, what a bank records and why the timing of payments matters.

Money is useful because people accept it

Money allows us to pay for goods and services without exchanging one item directly for another. Instead of offering a sack of rice in exchange for a repair, the kitchen can agree on a price in pounds and pay the repairer. Money also gives us a way to compare prices and keep financial records.

A currency is the monetary unit used for a payment. In the United Kingdom the currency is the pound sterling (GBP); in the United States it is the US dollar (USD). A number on its own is not a complete financial amount: 100 might mean £100, $100 or something else entirely.

A bank account is a record maintained by a bank. If the kitchen has a positive deposit balance of £2,000, the bank generally owes that sum to the account holder, subject to the account terms and applicable rules. The kitchen sees an asset: money it has a claim to. The bank records a liability: money it owes its depositor. These are two views of the same relationship.

Banks do more than store records. They facilitate payments, provide loans and other financial services, and manage their own funding and risks. The exact services depend on the bank and its licences.

Follow one day's cash

On Monday morning, our kitchen's account shows £2,000. A supporter sends £500, and the kitchen pays £750 for food. If both transactions settle that day, the closing balance is:

Movement Change Account balance
Opening balance — £2,000
Donation received +£500 £2,500
Food paid for −£750 £1,750

We can check the arithmetic ourselves: £2,000 + £500 − £750 = £1,750.

There is an important difference between a payment we expect and money the bank has actually received. Suppose the supporter promises £500 on Monday but only sends it on Thursday. We must not spend Monday as if that money has arrived. A future promise belongs in a forecast; confirmed settled cash belongs in the actual balance.

A bank can also show a pending card payment, a hold or a credit facility. This means the balance displayed on a screen may differ from the amount available for an immediate payment. Always ask which balance is being shown.

Cash is not the same as profit

Our kitchen may receive food on credit and pay the supplier next month. The expense may belong to this month's accounts even though the cash has not left yet. Conversely, repaying the principal of a loan uses cash without necessarily being an expense in the profit calculation.

For now, remember two questions:

  • Accounting: What has the organisation earned, spent, owned or owed?
  • Treasury: Will enough cash be available in the correct currency and account when a payment must be made?

The questions are connected, but they are not identical.

Try it yourself

Use a notebook or calculator; you do not need a computer or a real bank account.

  1. Start with £1,200 in a fictional account. Add a £300 receipt and subtract a £450 payment. Write the closing balance.
  2. Change the story: the £300 is only promised, not yet received. How much settled cash remains after the payment?
  3. Explain why adding a US-dollar receipt directly to a pound balance would be misleading.

Check your answers. The first balance is £1,050. Without the promised receipt, it is £750. GBP and USD must stay separate unless a clearly identified exchange rate is applied.
