Count, group and keep track of units

Course contents Next lesson — Describe a part in three ways

What you will learn

Read place value and use addition, subtraction, multiplication and division while keeping track of units.

Count one kind of thing at a time

A unit tells you what a number counts. A table with 12 seats has 12 seats, and 12 cents describes money. Write the unit each time before deciding which quantities you can add.

The number 263 has two hundreds, six tens and three ones. Each digit tells you how many groups of that size you have. In this course, one dollar contains 100 cents, so 263 cents is $2.63. The two digits after the decimal point show the cents left after grouping the dollars.

Work it through

  1. Draw three groups of four marks. Each mark stands for one seat. Count 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 seats. The short way to write equal groups is 3 × 4 = 12.
  2. Cross out five occupied seats. 12 − 5 = 7 seats remain. Add the five to the seven to check you return to 12.
  3. Put the 12 seat marks into three equal groups. 12 ÷ 3 = 4 seats in each group. Check by multiplying 4 by 3.
  4. Two workshop tickets cost 275 cents and 325 cents. Add them to get 600 cents, which is $6.00. Write the exact cents on the receipt before displaying dollars.

Try it and check

Divide 14 labels equally between four folders. How many whole labels go in each folder, and what remains? Then find the combined cost of a 450-cent booklet and a 125-cent badge.

Check: Three whole labels in each folder, with two remaining. Check that 4 × 3 + 2 = 14. The cost is 575 cents or $5.75. Put the unit next to each answer if you accidentally combine labels and cents.

Apply it to an Umicom project

Sketch an Umicom Studio practice receipt with fields for label, quantity and amount in cents. Write one expected total beside it. You will later compare that paper total with a small program. Start with the paper version today.
