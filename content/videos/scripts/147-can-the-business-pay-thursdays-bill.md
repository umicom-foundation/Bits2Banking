# Episode 147 — Can the business pay Thursday's bill?

*Treasury From Zero · Spoken lesson with a cash-ladder demonstration · Approximately 10–12 minutes*

## Opening: a perfectly ordinary problem

**On screen:** A small company, boxes ready for delivery, an invoice marked “payment expected Tuesday” and a wage bill due Wednesday.

**Narration:**

“Imagine that you run a business that sells equipment. Customers are buying your products, and you have invoices worth thousands of pounds. The business sounds successful. Then Thursday arrives, and a supplier needs to be paid. You open the bank account and realise that the money you expected from a customer hasn't arrived. Sales are good, but the cash isn't where you need it.

“This is one of the simplest—and most important—problems that treasury management helps us understand. Today we are not learning complicated finance. We are asking an ordinary question: what money is available, and will it be there on the day we need it?”

## Put the week's movements on paper

**On screen:** Write ‘Opening cash: £12,000’ on a blank page. Add five rows, one for each working day.

**Narration:**

“Let us call our fictional business Harbour Supplies. On Monday it starts with twelve thousand pounds. It must pay rent of four thousand five hundred pounds. A customer is expected to pay six thousand five hundred pounds on Tuesday. Wages of seven thousand three hundred pounds are due on Wednesday. A supplier needs three thousand five hundred pounds on Thursday. A four-thousand-pound grant might arrive on Friday.

“We cannot put all these numbers into one total and forget the dates. A payment arriving on Friday cannot help us pay a supplier on Thursday. The date is part of the financial information.”

**On screen:** Fill in the cash-ladder table, pausing after each day. Show the formula:

> Closing cash = opening cash + money received − money paid.

**Narration:**

“Monday closes at seven thousand five hundred pounds. If Tuesday's payment arrives, Tuesday closes at fourteen thousand. Wednesday closes at six thousand seven hundred. Thursday falls to three thousand two hundred. Friday, assuming the grant arrives, ends at seven thousand two hundred.

“The lowest point is Thursday—not Friday. If the company wants to keep at least five thousand pounds available as a safety buffer, Thursday is a warning even though the balance remains positive.”

## Change just one assumption

**On screen:** Cross out Tuesday's £6,500 receipt and write “Expected following Monday”. Redraw the balance path.

**Narration:**

“Now imagine the customer is late. The company's Thursday forecast becomes **minus three thousand three hundred pounds**. That's not a bank balance we can simply assume is allowed. It means the planned payments cannot all be covered by the cash forecast unless something changes.

“This is why a forecast is not a promise. A promised payment, an approved payment instruction and settled money in the account are different states. A useful treasury report has to make those differences visible.”

## What a treasury team can do

**On screen:** Show three cards: Confirm the receipt; Ask for an agreed supplier date; Review authorised funding.

**Narration:**

“The treasury team first checks what is actually known. Has the customer initiated the payment? Is the due date correct? Is there an agreed credit facility? Could the supplier accept a different date? What approvals would be needed?

“Notice that none of those questions involves pretending the money arrived or hiding a missing payment. A Treasury Management System helps us record the possible actions and their consequences. It doesn't replace judgement or permission.”

## Viewer's exercise

**On screen:** Display this question for thirty seconds:

> If the customer is late and Thursday closes at −£3,300, how much additional money would be needed to reach a £5,000 safety buffer?

**Narration:**

“Subtract the forecast from the target: five thousand minus negative three thousand three hundred is **eight thousand three hundred pounds**. Think about why that is different from the three thousand three hundred needed merely to reach zero.”

## Closing

**Narration:**

“Today you built a cash ladder. You did not need a bank terminal, a Java program or a complicated formula. Yet you have already learned something used by real treasury professionals: **the right amount on the wrong day is still a problem**.

“In the written lesson, try changing the dates yourself and explain the result in ordinary English. Next we will explore deposits and loans, where cash moves today and a repayment or interest amount becomes due later.”
