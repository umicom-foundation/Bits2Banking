# 5. Who does what? Front office, middle office and back office

*Bits to Banking · Treasury Management, TMS and Calypso From Zero · Umicom Foundation*

Imagine that a company must pay a supplier in US dollars in three months. The company keeps most of its money in pounds, so it agrees with a bank to exchange pounds for dollars on a future date.

The agreement may take only a few minutes. Making sure that it is fair, authorised, confirmed and eventually settled can involve several different teams. Financial organisations often describe them as the **front office**, **middle office** and **back office**.

These terms describe responsibilities and controls. They do not necessarily mean three separate rooms or buildings.

## The front office: agreeing the transaction

The front office deals with markets, customers and trading decisions. It may quote a price, negotiate a transaction, execute it and capture its terms in the trading system.

For our example, suppose the company agrees to buy **USD 125,000** and sell **GBP 100,000** in three months. The agreed rate is **1.25 US dollars per pound**. The front office must record the correct direction, currencies, amounts, counterparty and contractual date.

It is not enough to write “FX 125,000”. A colleague must be able to tell which currency is bought and which is sold, and by whom.

## The middle office: independent checks

The middle office usually provides controls such as risk measurement, trade verification, valuation review and limits. It may check whether the agreed terms match the recorded transaction, whether the counterparty and book are permitted, and whether the deal stays within risk limits.

Imagine that the front office accidentally enters **USD 1,250,000** instead of USD 125,000. An independent verification or limit check could detect a mismatch before the error affects payments and financial reporting.

The purpose is not to slow down every transaction. It is to ensure the person creating a commitment does not have unrestricted authority to approve all of its consequences.

## The back office: making the agreement work

The back office handles confirmations, settlement instructions, payment processing, matching, reconciliations and exceptions. It makes sure the agreed exchange is actually carried out.

Our FX forward has two expected currency movements on its settlement date:

| Our company's perspective | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Receive from counterparty | USD 125,000 |
| Pay to counterparty | GBP 100,000 |

A back-office employee might check the bank accounts and settlement instructions, confirm the deal terms with the counterparty, and later investigate a missing incoming dollar payment. Sending a payment instruction is not the same as receiving the bank's confirmation of settlement.

## What does treasury do alongside them?

Treasury asks whether the company will have GBP 100,000 available on the day it must pay. The deal might reduce currency uncertainty, but it still creates a funding obligation. Treasury needs to plan for it.

Finance and accounting also have work to do. They record the financial effects in the appropriate books and reconcile them to the ledger. A single transaction therefore has several views: agreed economics, risk, operational processing, cash and accounting.

## Follow one transaction on paper

Draw five boxes labelled **Front Office**, **Middle Office**, **Back Office**, **Treasury**, and **Finance**. Place these tasks in the correct boxes: negotiate the FX rate; check a trading limit; confirm settlement accounts; forecast the GBP payment; reconcile accounting entries.

**Check:** Negotiation belongs primarily to the front office, risk limit checking to the middle office, settlement details to the back office, cash forecasting to treasury, and ledger reconciliation to finance. Real organisations may divide responsibilities differently, but the segregation-of-duties principle remains.

**Question to discuss:** Why might it be unsafe for one trader to negotiate a deal, approve their own exception and send the money without another check?

A sensible answer mentions the chance of mistakes, unauthorised actions and undetected fraud, not merely the number of staff involved.
