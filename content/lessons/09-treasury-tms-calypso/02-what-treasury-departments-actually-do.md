# 2. Treasury: making sure payments can be made on time

*Bits to Banking · Treasury Management, TMS and Calypso From Zero · Umicom Foundation*

The community kitchen from Chapter 1 is now organising a week's worth of meals. Its manager has an encouraging problem: donations are expected, but the food supplier wants payment before some donations arrive.

That timing problem is a simple version of **treasury management**. Large companies, charities and banks face it every day, often across hundreds of accounts and several currencies.

## The problem with looking at just one balance

Suppose the kitchen begins Monday with £5,000. A supplier must be paid £6,000 on Tuesday. A supporter expects to send £1,200 on Thursday. A utility bill of £500 is due on Friday.

A useful treasury tool is a **cash ladder**: a list of expected money coming in and going out, organised by date. It answers a question that a single balance cannot answer: *when might the money run short?*

| Date | Expected money in | Money out | Forecast balance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Monday, opening | — | — | £5,000 |
| Tuesday | — | £6,000 | **−£1,000** |
| Thursday | £1,200 | — | £200 |
| Friday | — | £500 | **−£300** |

Notice that the final balance is not the only concern. The organisation is already short by £1,000 on Tuesday, even though funds are expected on Thursday. Waiting for the Thursday donation will not pay Tuesday's supplier.

The negative figures are **forecast funding gaps**. They do not mean the bank has allowed an unauthorised overdraft. They tell us that the current plan cannot be completed as written.

## What can the treasury team do?

The first task is to check the facts. Is the £6,000 due on Tuesday? Can the supplier accept two payments? Is the donation confirmed, or only likely? Is there another account with available funds? Only then should the treasury team choose a response.

A treasurer might arrange an authorised short-term facility, transfer money from another account, postpone a discretionary purchase or agree different payment terms. Each choice has a cost and an approval requirement. Treasury should not simply invent a receipt to make a spreadsheet look positive.

In a larger organisation, treasury also:

- Watches balances and expected movements across bank accounts and currencies.
- Decides how to fund a shortage or use a temporary surplus.
- Manages exposure to changing interest rates and foreign-exchange rates.
- Coordinates debt, investments, collateral and liquidity limits.
- Provides evidence to finance, risk management and senior decision-makers.

A **surplus** means more available cash than needed for the planned obligations. A **shortfall** means the opposite. **Liquidity** is the practical ability to meet obligations when due, not simply the size of a company's assets.

## Forecasts need confidence labels

Now suppose a supporter says, “I hope to donate £1,200 on Thursday.” That money should be described as *forecast* or *unconfirmed*. If the supporter has already sent it and the bank has credited the account, it is *settled* or *available*, depending on account restrictions. Those states should not be merged in a single total without explanation.

A credible forecast records the amount, currency, account, expected date, source and confidence. It is updated when new information arrives. The treasurer can then see a safer scenario in which the donation is delayed or smaller than expected.

## Practice: prepare a funding note

Open a new page and copy the four-row cash ladder above. Then answer these questions in ordinary language.

1. What is the first day with a funding gap, and how large is it?
2. If the £1,200 donation is delayed until the following Monday, what is Friday's forecast balance?
3. What information would you check before asking a bank for temporary funding?

**Check your reasoning.** The first gap is £1,000 on Tuesday. Without Thursday's receipt, Friday would show £5,000 − £6,000 − £500 = **−£1,500**. A funding decision needs the confirmed due dates, available accounts, borrowing permissions, expected receipts and cost.

A treasury management system automates much of this record-keeping, but people still decide which assumptions and actions are acceptable. Chapter 3 explains why an organisation may outgrow a spreadsheet.
